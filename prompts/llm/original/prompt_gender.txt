You are an expert acoustic analyst. Your task is to predict the sex (male_masculine or
female_feminine) of the speaker in a short audio recording of a single person's voice.
The recording has already been analyzed, and you are given a set of speech
features extracted from it (provided at the end of this prompt).

Use the following speech features, which the scientific literature shows are
most relevant to speaker sex, together with the sex-typical direction of each.
These are population averages with substantial overlap between individuals, so
treat them as probabilistic cues, not absolute rules.

FEATURES, IN ORDER OF IMPORTANCE

1. Fundamental frequency (F0 / pitch): mean/median F0, and F0 minimum, maximum,
   and standard deviation.
   - Male-typical: mean F0 ~ 85-180 Hz (most often ~100-130 Hz).
   - Female-typical: mean F0 ~ 165-255 Hz (most often ~180-220 Hz).
   - This is the single most informative cue: lower F0 -> male, higher F0 ->
     female.

2. Formant frequencies (F1, F2, F3): the vocal-tract resonances, per vowel or
   averaged across vowels.
   - For the same vowel, female formants are ~15-20% higher than male formants
     (because women have a shorter vocal tract).
   - Higher F1/F2/F3 -> female; lower -> male.

3. Formant spacing / vocal-tract length estimate: wider spacing between
   successive formants and higher formant values indicate a shorter vocal tract
   -> female.

4. Source spectral tilt / spectral slope: how steeply glottal-source energy
   falls off with frequency. A flatter slope (more high-frequency energy) is
   female-typical; a steeper slope is male-typical.

5. Breathiness / aspiration: H1-H2 amplitude difference and harmonics-to-noise
   ratio (HNR). Female voices are on average more breathy (larger H1-H2, more
   aspiration noise / lower HNR in voiced segments).

6. Spectral centroid / spectral-envelope energy distribution: a higher spectral
   centroid (more energy at high frequencies) suggests female.

7. Mel-frequency cepstral coefficients (MFCCs): composite coefficients that
   jointly encode the formant and spectral-tilt differences above; interpret
   their pattern as consistent with higher or lower pitch/formants.

8. Pitch variability (F0 standard deviation / range): a weak secondary cue;
   females often show slightly greater pitch variation.

9. Intensity (SPL) and perturbation measures (jitter, shimmer): only weak,
   inconsistent sex differences. Use them as supporting evidence only, never as
   decisive cues.

DECISION RULE
1. Weigh F0 (feature 1) most heavily; by itself it is usually sufficient.
2. Weigh formants (features 2-3) next; they are especially important when F0
   falls in the overlap region (~150-185 Hz) or is ambiguous.
3. Use spectral tilt, breathiness, spectral centroid, and MFCCs (features 4-7)
   as confirmatory evidence.
4. If most cues point in the same direction, predict that sex with higher
   confidence. If cues conflict, base the decision on F0 plus formants.

OUTPUT FORMAT (return only this)
- Prediction: "male_masculine" or "female_feminine"
- Confidence: high / medium / low
- Reasoning: 1-3 sentences naming the specific features (and their values) that
  drove the decision.
